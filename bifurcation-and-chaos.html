Bifurcation & Chaos

From a population of rabbits to the butterfly effect: what happens when you turn one knob in a simple equation.

Mar 22, 2025 · 18 min read

      A Little Bit of Context

      It started with one equation. A single line of algebra, something you could put on a postcard, produces stable populations, oscillations, and pure randomness, depending on one number. Once I saw that, I went down the rabbit hole of chaos theory and haven't really come out.

      That number is the parameter, and the study of how a system's long-run behaviour changes as you slowly turn a parameter is called bifurcation theory. For my B.Tech project I picked two classic examples. The first is the logistic map, a discrete model that is the gentlest introduction to the subject. The second is the Lorenz system, a set of three ODEs that is the textbook example of chaos in continuous time. This post is the long-form version of that project. I also put in what I learned reading around it, and I fix a few mistakes I made along the way.

      I assume you are comfortable with derivatives and know what an ODE is. You don't need a dynamical systems course.

      What is a Bifurcation?

      Take any system whose state evolves by a rule that depends on a parameter \(r\). The rule can be continuous (a flow) or discrete (a map):

      $$\dot{x} = f(x, r) \qquad \text{or} \qquad x_{n+1} = f(x_n, r).$$

      For most values of \(r\), nudging \(r\) slightly changes the picture only slightly. Equilibria drift a little and orbits stretch a little. At special values, the picture changes qualitatively. Equilibria are born or destroyed, stability is exchanged, or an oscillation appears out of nowhere. Those special values are bifurcation points. A bifurcation is a change in the type of behaviour, not just its size.

      Fixed Points & Stability

      The first thing to find in any system is where it can sit still. For a flow these are points with \(f(x^*) = 0\). For a map they are points with \(f(x^*) = x^*\). Then we ask whether a small perturbation dies out or grows. Linearising around \(x^*\) answers it:

      $$\underbrace{|f'(x^*)| < 1}_{\text{map: stable}} \qquad\qquad \underbrace{f'(x^*) < 0}_{\text{flow: stable}}$$

      In higher dimensions \(f'\) becomes the Jacobian matrix \(J\), and the question becomes where its eigenvalues lie. For a flow, a fixed point is stable when every eigenvalue has a negative real part. For a map, it is stable when every eigenvalue lies inside the unit circle. A bifurcation happens exactly when an eigenvalue crosses that boundary as \(r\) changes. How it crosses decides which bifurcation you get.

      The Classic Bifurcations

      Near a bifurcation point, almost any system looks like one of a handful of simple "normal forms". Learn these and you can recognise the local behaviour of most systems you'll meet.

      TypeNormal formWhat happensEigenvalue picture
      Saddle-node\(\dot x = r + x^2\)Two fixed points collide and annihilatereal λ through 0 (map: +1)
      Transcritical\(\dot x = rx - x^2\)Two fixed points cross and swap stabilityreal λ through 0 (map: +1)
      Pitchfork\(\dot x = rx - x^3\)One fixed point splits into three (symmetric systems)real λ through 0 (map: +1)

      Each of these also comes in a supercritical flavour and a subcritical flavour. In the supercritical case (shown above) the new state is born stable and grows smoothly from zero, so the transition is gentle. In the subcritical case the new branches are unstable and the system jumps somewhere far away. Keep that in mind, because the Lorenz system's Hopf bifurcation turns out to be the dangerous, subcritical kind.

      1.Strogatz's Nonlinear Dynamics and Chaos, chapters 3 and 8, is the best place to see each normal form worked out with pictures.

      The Logistic Map

      Rabbits & Feedback

      Suppose \(x_n\) is the rabbit population this year and we want next year's. The simplest guess is \(x_{n+1} = r\,x_n\), where \(r\) is the growth rate. With \(r = 2\) the population doubles every year, which is exponential growth, and no real population does that forever. The environment pushes back, so we add a constraint:

      $$x_{n+1} = r\,x_n\,(1 - x_n).$$

      Here \(x\) is a fraction of the theoretical maximum, so it lives in \([0, 1]\). As \(x\) approaches 1, the factor \((1-x)\) approaches 0 and chokes the growth. This is a negative feedback loop. The bigger the population gets this year, the smaller it tends to be the next. Plot \(x_{n+1}\) against \(x_n\) and you get an inverted parabola, a single hump. That's the whole model. Robert May popularised it in 1976 as a warning that even simple ecological models can behave in wildly complicated ways[2].

      2.R. M. May, "Simple mathematical models with very complicated dynamics", Nature 261 (1976). It is still very readable.

      Try \(r = 2.6\) starting from \(x_0 = 0.4\):

      The population wobbles and then settles. This matches what we see in the wild, where populations often hold steady as long as births and deaths balance. Change \(x_0\) and the first few years change, but the long-run value stays the same. That long-run value is a stable fixed point.

      The Rabbit Island

      Before doing any algebra, here's the whole story told with rabbits. Picture a small island with plenty of grass, no foxes, and a colony of rabbits. Every spring we count them. The only thing we're allowed to change is \(r\), how fast they breed. Each value of \(r\) below links to the explorer further down, so you can watch that year-by-year count yourself.

      1. Too slow to survive (r = 0.8). Each generation leaves fewer babies than there were parents. The count shrinks every spring until the island is empty. However many rabbits we start with, the colony goes extinct.
      2. A quiet balance (r = 2.6). The colony grows, overshoots a little, and settles at about 61% of what the island can hold. Births and deaths balance and every spring's count is the same. Start with 10 rabbits or 1,000 and you still end up there. This is what we usually picture when we think of nature in balance.
      3. Boom and bust (r = 3.2). Now they breed so fast that a good year eats the island bare. The next spring there are fewer rabbits, the grass grows back, and the year after that the colony booms again. The count flips between a high value and a low one forever. The colony never settles, but it is perfectly regular, with a two-year cycle.
      4. A four-year rhythm (r = 3.5). Breed a little faster and the boom-bust cycle itself splits. Now there's a big boom, a crash, a smaller boom and a deeper crash, and then the pattern repeats. A bit faster still and you get 8 years, then 16, then 32, with each new split arriving sooner than the last.
      5. No pattern at all (r = 3.9). Past about \(r = 3.57\) the springs never repeat. One year there are lots of rabbits, the next almost none, then a middling year, with no rhythm you could ever learn. Nothing random is happening, though. The same rule produces every year from the one before. If two rangers started counting from colonies that differed by a single rabbit, their records would agree for a few years and then have nothing to do with each other.
      6. Order returns, briefly (r = 3.83). In the middle of the chaos there's a narrow band of breeding rates where the colony locks into a clean three-year cycle again. Nudge \(r\) slightly and it dissolves back into chaos.

      That's the entire plot of this post. The island never changed, the rule never changed, and no randomness was added. We only turned one knob. Every change in behaviour along the way is a bifurcation, and the last stage is chaos. The rest of the post explains why each change happens and where it happens.

      Is this real? Real rabbits have foxes, winters and diseases, so no wild colony follows this exact rule. Boom-and-bust cycles are real, though. A famous example comes from the Hudson's Bay Company fur-trapping records, which show snowshoe hare numbers in Canada rising and crashing roughly every ten years for more than a century. In the 1990s, experiments on lab colonies of flour beetles changed one demographic parameter by hand. The researchers watched the populations move from a steady state to cycles and then to chaotic swings, just as the models predicted.

      Fixed Points by Hand

      Setting \(x^* = r x^* (1 - x^*)\) gives two fixed points, \(x^* = 0\) (extinction) and \(x^* = 1 - 1/r\). Their stability comes from \(f'(x) = r(1 - 2x)\):

      $$f'(0) = r, \qquad f'\!\left(1 - \tfrac{1}{r}\right) = 2 - r.$$

      So extinction is stable when \(r < 1\). The positive equilibrium is stable when \(|2 - r| < 1\), that is \(1 < r < 3\). At \(r = 1\) the two fixed points pass through each other and swap stability, which is a textbook transcritical bifurcation. For \(r = 2.6\) we get \(1 - 1/2.6 = 0.6154\), exactly the value the table converged to.

      At \(r = 3\) the multiplier \(2 - r\) hits \(-1\). By the table in the last section, that's the signature of a period-doubling bifurcation. Past \(r = 3\), the population stops settling on one value and instead oscillates between two:

      $$x_\pm = \frac{(r+1) \pm \sqrt{(r-3)(r+1)}}{2r}, \qquad \text{stable for } 3 < r < 1 + \sqrt 6 \approx 3.449.$$

      Period Doubling

      One year the population is high, the next it's low, and then the cycle repeats. That kind of boom-and-bust cycle is observed in real animal populations too. Push \(r\) higher and each branch splits again, giving a 4-year cycle, then 8, 16, 32 and on. The splits arrive faster and faster until, around \(r \approx 3.5699\), the period becomes infinite. The population never repeats. Play with it:

      At \(r = 3.7\) or \(4\) the series looks like noise, but nothing random is going on. Every value follows exactly from the one before it. This is deterministic chaos. The randomness is convincing enough that Ulam and von Neumann suggested the \(r = 4\) map as a random number generator back in 1947. The catch is that if you know the exact initial condition you can reproduce every value, which makes them pseudo-random.

      The Bifurcation Diagram

      To see every value of \(r\) at once, iterate the map for a long time, throw away the transient, and plot the values the orbit keeps visiting against \(r\). This is my BTP code, lightly tidied:

      import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def logistic_map(r, x):
    return r * x * (1 - x)

r_values   = np.linspace(0, 4, 1000)   # 1000 values of r, all iterated at once
iterations = 1000                      # total iterations per r
last       = 100                       # only keep the last 100 (post-transient)

x = 1e-5 * np.ones(len(r_values))      # same tiny initial population for every r
results = []

for i in range(iterations):
    x = logistic_map(r_values, x)      # vectorised: one step for all r
    if i >= iterations - last:
        results.append(np.copy(x))

results    = np.array(results).flatten()
r_repeated = np.tile(r_values, last)

plt.figure(figsize=(12, 6))
plt.plot(r_repeated, results, ',k', alpha=0.25)   # ',' = pixel markers
plt.title("Bifurcation Diagram of the Logistic Map")
plt.xlabel("Growth Rate (r)")
plt.ylabel("Population (x)")
plt.grid(alpha=0.3)
plt.show()

      Read it left to right. You see a single branch (fixed point), a fork at \(r = 3\) (period 2), forks on forks (4, 8, 16, …), then a dark cloud where chaos begins. The equilibrium curve rising from \(r = 1\) is the \(1 - 1/r\) we computed by hand.

      Windows of Order

      Just when you'd expect chaos from here on, order returns. Inside the cloud there are clean white windows of periodic behaviour. The biggest opens at \(r = 1 + \sqrt 8 \approx 3.8284\) with a stable period-3 cycle. It is born in a saddle-node (tangent) bifurcation. That window then period-doubles to 6, 12, 24, … and dissolves back into chaos. Click "Zoom inside the window" above and you'll see a miniature copy of the whole diagram. The large-scale structure repeats on smaller and smaller scales. The diagram is a fractal.

      Period 3 is special. A theorem by Li and Yorke, titled Period Three Implies Chaos[4], says that a continuous map of an interval with a period-3 orbit has orbits of every period, plus uncountably many orbits that are never periodic. (Sharkovskii had proved the "every period" part even earlier.) So the logistic map contains cycles of length 37, 50, 1052, whatever you like, if you pick the right \(r\).

      4.T.-Y. Li & J. A. Yorke, Amer. Math. Monthly 82 (1975). This paper is where the word "chaos" entered mathematics.

      Feigenbaum's Constant

      In the mid-1970s the physicist Mitchell Feigenbaum computed where the period-doublings happen, famously on a pocket calculator. He then divided the width of each bifurcation interval by the width of the next one[3]:

      $$\delta = \lim_{n\to\infty} \frac{r_n - r_{n-1}}{r_{n+1} - r_n} = 4.669\,201\,609\ldots$$

      The bifurcations come faster and faster, but the ratio between successive gaps closes in on a fixed number. It doesn't match any other known constant, so it seems to be a new fundamental constant of mathematics. A second constant, \(\alpha \approx 2.5029\), describes how the widths of the forks shrink.

      3.M. J. Feigenbaum, "Quantitative universality for a class of nonlinear transformations", J. Stat. Phys. 19 (1978).

      Universality

      What's even crazier is that \(\delta\) has nothing to do with the logistic map in particular. Take any map with a single smooth hump, for example \(x_{n+1} = r\sin(\pi x_n)\), and iterate it. You'll see the same cascade of period-doublings, and the ratio will converge to the same 4.669. That's why it shows up in real experiments that have nothing to do with rabbits:

      • Convection in a fluid. Libchaber's experiments on a fluid heated from below, around 1980, saw period-doubling in the temperature oscillations. The measured ratios came out close to Feigenbaum's number.
      • Dripping faucets. Open the tap slightly more and the drops start coming in pairs, then in fours, and eventually chaotically, all just by adjusting the flow rate.
      • Our eyes and flickering lights. Above a certain flicker rate, responses in the visual system lock to every other flash, which is period doubling in neurons.
      • Electronic circuits, lasers, heart cells, and the firing of neurons in your brain.

      The Mandelbrot Connection

      Arguably the most famous fractal is the Mandelbrot set, built from \(z_{n+1} = z_n^2 + c\). The plot twist is that the logistic bifurcation diagram is part of the Mandelbrot set. The substitution \(z = r\left(\tfrac12 - x\right)\) turns the logistic map exactly into

      $$z_{n+1} = z_n^2 + c, \qquad c = \frac{r}{2} - \frac{r^2}{4}.$$

      As \(r\) runs from 1 to 4, \(c\) runs along the real axis from \(\tfrac14\) to \(-2\). The fork at \(r = 3\) lands at \(c = -0.75\), exactly where the main cardioid meets the period-2 bulb. The period-3 window lands at \(c = -1.75\), on the tiny "mini-Mandelbrot" sitting on the real axis. The bulbs along the axis are the period-doublings, seen from a different angle.

      Chaos & the Butterfly Effect

      Lorenz's Rounding Error

      In 1961 the meteorologist Edward Lorenz was running a 12-variable weather model on a Royal McBee computer. He wanted to look at one run again, so he restarted it from the middle, typing in a value from a printout. The printout showed 0.506, but the machine stored 0.506127. The difference was about one part in four thousand. He went for a coffee, came back, and the new forecast had diverged completely from the old one[6].

      This is sensitive dependence on initial conditions (SDIC), and it became famous through the title of his 1972 talk: "Does the flap of a butterfly's wings in Brazil set off a tornado in Texas?" The same thing happens in the logistic map. The figure below runs it twice, once from Lorenz's \(0.506127\) and once from his rounded \(0.506\):

      That last point is the real lesson. Making your measurement a thousand times more accurate doesn't buy a thousand times more forecast. It buys a fixed number of extra steps. This is why weather forecasts beyond about two weeks are hopeless no matter how big the supercomputer is.

      Lyapunov Exponents

      We can measure SDIC. If a tiny gap grows like \(\delta_n \approx \delta_0\, e^{\lambda n}\), then \(\lambda\) is the Lyapunov exponent. For a 1-D map, the chain rule turns the growth of the gap into a product of slopes, so

      $$\lambda = \lim_{n\to\infty} \frac1n \sum_{i=0}^{n-1} \ln\big|f'(x_i)\big| = \lim_{n\to\infty} \frac1n \sum_{i=0}^{n-1} \ln\big|r(1 - 2x_i)\big|.$$

      \(\lambda < 0\) means nearby orbits converge, so the motion is a stable fixed point or cycle. \(\lambda > 0\) means they separate exponentially, which is chaos. The time \(1/\lambda\) is the "Lyapunov time", the horizon beyond which prediction fails. For \(r = 4\) one can show \(\lambda = \ln 2\) exactly, so every iteration loses one bit of information about the starting point. The readout under Figure 2 computes \(\lambda\) live, so try \(r = 3.83\) (window, \(\lambda < 0\)) against \(r = 3.9\) (chaos, \(\lambda > 0\)).

      Chaos, defined. Aperiodic long-term behaviour, in a deterministic system, that shows sensitive dependence on initial conditions. All three parts matter. Noise isn't chaos, and neither is merely complicated periodic motion.

      The Lorenz System

      In 1963 Lorenz cut a model of convection, a fluid heated from below, down to three ODEs[5]:

      $$\begin{aligned} \dot x &= \sigma (y - x), \\ \dot y &= x(\rho - z) - y, \\ \dot z &= xy - \beta z. \end{aligned}$$
      5.E. N. Lorenz, "Deterministic Nonperiodic Flow", J. Atmos. Sci. 20 (1963), one of the most cited papers in all of physics.

      Roughly, \(x\) is the speed of the convective roll, \(y\) is the temperature difference between rising and falling fluid, and \(z\) is the distortion of the vertical temperature profile. \(\sigma\) is the Prandtl number, \(\beta\) is a geometric factor, and \(\rho\) is the Rayleigh number, which measures how hard we heat the fluid. I keep Lorenz's classic \(\sigma = 10,\ \beta = 8/3\) and use \(\rho\) as the bifurcation parameter. One more useful fact is that \(\nabla\!\cdot\! f = -(\sigma + 1 + \beta) \approx -13.7 < 0\). Volumes in phase space shrink, so all trajectories end up on a set of zero volume, the attractor.

      Equilibria & Stability

      The origin is always an equilibrium, meaning no convection. For \(\rho > 1\) two more appear, the steady clockwise and anticlockwise rolls:

      $$C^\pm = \Big(\pm\sqrt{\beta(\rho - 1)},\ \pm\sqrt{\beta(\rho - 1)},\ \rho - 1\Big).$$

      The Jacobian at the origin has eigenvalues \(-\beta\) and \(\tfrac12\big[-(\sigma+1) \pm \sqrt{(\sigma+1)^2 + 4\sigma(\rho - 1)}\big]\). One of them turns positive exactly at \(\rho = 1\), which is a pitchfork bifurcation (the system is symmetric under \((x,y) \to (-x,-y)\)). Working out the characteristic polynomial at \(C^\pm\) shows that a complex pair crosses the imaginary axis at

      $$\rho_H = \frac{\sigma(\sigma + \beta + 3)}{\sigma - \beta - 1} = \frac{470}{19} \approx 24.74,$$

      which is a Hopf bifurcation. Unlike the gentle picture in Figure 1, this one is subcritical. The unstable limit cycles shrink onto \(C^\pm\) and swallow them, so there's no small stable oscillation to fall back on, and the trajectory has nowhere to go but the strange attractor.

      Simulating the Attractor

      My BTP code integrates the system with SciPy's solve_ivp for three values of \(\rho\):

      import numpy as np
from scipy.integrate import solve_ivp
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def lorenz(t, state, sigma, beta, rho):
    x, y, z = state
    return [sigma * (y - x),
            x * (rho - z) - y,
            x * y - beta * z]

sigma, beta = 10.0, 8.0 / 3.0
initial_state = [1.0, 1.0, 1.0]
t_span = (0, 50)
t_eval = np.linspace(*t_span, 10000)

for rho in [10, 28, 50]:
    sol = solve_ivp(lorenz, t_span, initial_state, t_eval=t_eval,
                    args=(sigma, beta, rho), rtol=1e-9, atol=1e-9)
    x, y, z = sol.y

    ax = plt.figure(figsize=(10, 6)).add_subplot(projection="3d")
    ax.plot(x, y, z, lw=0.5)
    ax.set_title(f"Lorenz System (rho={rho})")
    ax.set_xlabel("X"); ax.set_ylabel("Y"); ax.set_zlabel("Z")
    plt.show()

      At \(\rho = 10\) the trajectory spirals into one of the steady rolls. At \(\rho = 28\) it never settles. It loops around one lobe a few times, jumps unpredictably to the other, and traces the famous butterfly without ever crossing itself or repeating. That's the strange attractor, a fractal object with dimension ≈ 2.06, more than a surface but less than a solid.

      A Bifurcation Diagram for Lorenz

      Turning a flow into a bifurcation diagram needs one extra idea. In my original BTP code I plotted the last 500 samples of \(z(t)\) for each \(\rho\). That works, but it smears the whole range of \(z\) into a vertical line whenever there is any oscillation, so periodic and chaotic motion look alike. A better trick, which Lorenz himself used, is to record only the successive local maxima of \(z(t)\). This turns the flow into a map. A periodic orbit then gives a few dots, chaos gives a cloud, and a fixed point gives a single point at \(z = \rho - 1\).

      from scipy.signal import argrelmax

rhos, peaks = [], []
for rho in np.linspace(0, 50, 600):
    sol = solve_ivp(lorenz, (0, 80), [1.0, 1.0, 1.0], args=(sigma, beta, rho),
                    t_eval=np.linspace(40, 80, 16000),   # discard t < 40 as transient
                    rtol=1e-9, atol=1e-9)
    z = sol.y[2]
    zmax = z[argrelmax(z)[0]]
    if len(zmax) == 0:            # no oscillation left: we sat on a fixed point
        zmax = [z[-1]]
    rhos.extend([rho] * len(zmax))
    peaks.extend(zmax)

plt.scatter(rhos, peaks, s=0.3, c="k")
plt.xlabel("rho"); plt.ylabel("successive maxima of z")

      The picture tells the story in the table. A flat line at zero for \(\rho < 1\) is the pitchfork. Next comes the rising line \(z = \rho - 1\), the steady rolls. Then the cloud erupts near \(\rho \approx 24\). Switch to \([0, 250]\) and you'll find the white windows of periodic behaviour inside the chaos, the continuous-time cousins of the period-3 window in the logistic map.

      Bifurcations in the Wild

      Once you know the shapes, you start seeing them everywhere[7].

      • Physical systems: fluid convection and turbulence, climate tipping points, lasers switching to self-pulsing, chaotic electronic circuits.
      • Biological systems: population cycles and predator–prey oscillations, neurons firing (a Hopf bifurcation from rest to spiking), circadian rhythms, heart arrhythmias.
      • Chemistry: the Belousov–Zhabotinsky reaction, a chemical clock that oscillates between colours.
      • Engineering: buckling beams (pitchforks), flutter in aircraft wings (Hopf), voltage collapse in power grids (saddle-node), and stability margins in control systems.
      7.S. M. Farris & M. S. Hamdi, "Bifurcation Theory: A Review", RJCM 17(2), 2023. It has a long survey of applications across physics, biology, chemistry, medicine and geography.

      Hopf Bifurcation in Whale Populations

      One paper I read for the project shows how far these ideas stretch[8]. Liu, Wu and Zhang study an age-structured model of baleen whales, where \(u(t, a)\) is the density of whales of age \(a\) at time \(t\):

      $$\partial_t u + \partial_a u = -\mu u, \qquad u(t, 0) = \frac{\beta_0 \int_0^\infty \beta(a) u\, da}{1 + \alpha\left(\int_0^\infty \beta(a) u\, da\right)^3}.$$
      8.Z. Liu, Y. Wu, X. Zhang, "Existence of periodic wave trains for an age-structured model with diffusion", DCDS-B 26(12), 2021.

      Births follow a generalised Beverton–Holt law, so newborns saturate at high adult density. Whales only reproduce once they are older than a juvenile period \(\tau\). That \(\tau\) is the bifurcation parameter. After rescaling, the linearisation around the positive equilibrium reduces to a delay-type characteristic equation:

      $$\zeta + p_0 + q_0 e^{-\tau\zeta} = 0, \qquad p_0 = \mu,\quad q_0 = \frac{\mu(2\beta_0 R - 3)}{\beta_0 R}.$$

      The method is exactly the recipe from §2. You look for a purely imaginary root \(\zeta = i\omega\), which needs \(\beta_0 R > 3\). That gives \(\omega_0 = \sqrt{q_0^2 - p_0^2}\) and a critical \(\tau_0 = \tfrac{1}{\omega_0}\arccos(-p_0/q_0)\). Then you check the transversality condition \(\tfrac{d}{d\tau}\mathrm{Re}\,\zeta > 0\), which says the roots really do cross the axis. With their numbers (\(\mu = 0.2,\ \beta_0 = 4,\ R = 1\)) you get \(q_0 = 0.25\), \(\omega_0 = 0.15\) and \(\tau_0 \approx 16.65\). When the juvenile period is short (\(\tau = 10\)) the whale population settles down. When it is long (\(\tau = 20\)) the population oscillates forever. Long maturation delays destabilise populations, a Hopf bifurcation with a real ecological meaning. With spatial diffusion added, those oscillations become periodic wave trains travelling through space.

      Extra Notes

      • Tolerances matter. solve_ivp defaults to rtol=1e-3. For a chaotic system the integration error is itself a small perturbation that grows exponentially, so after a few Lyapunov times the trajectory you plot isn't the trajectory. Statistical features like the shape of the attractor survive, but individual paths don't. Use tight tolerances (or a symplectic or high-order method) when it matters.
      • Transients. My first Lorenz bifurcation code started every \(\rho\) from \((1,1,1)\) and kept only \(t \in [47.5, 50]\). Close to \(\rho_H\), convergence is very slow (the eigenvalues have nearly zero real part), so the diagram shows fuzzy branches that are really just slow decay. Longer transients sharpen it.
      • Bistability. For \(24.06 < \rho < 24.74\), whether you see chaos or a steady roll depends on the initial condition. Sweeping \(\rho\) up versus down (continuing from the last state) shows hysteresis, the fingerprint of a subcritical bifurcation.
      • from mpl_toolkits.mplot3d import Axes3D isn't needed on modern Matplotlib (≥ 3.2). add_subplot(projection="3d") just works.
      • A correction to something I read: the review paper attributes "Period Three Implies Chaos" to Feigenbaum. It is actually by Li and Yorke (1975). Feigenbaum's 1978 paper is the one about universality and δ.
      • In my BTP report I called the Lorenz transition a "butterfly bifurcation". That isn't a standard term. More precisely, the attractor appears through a homoclinic explosion (ρ ≈ 13.93) and a crisis (ρ ≈ 24.06), and the steady states die in a subcritical Hopf (ρ ≈ 24.74).
      • All the Python scripts from this post, along with my BTP report, are in the project repository on GitHub.
      • If you want to go further: Strogatz[1] chapters 9–10 cover exactly this material, and James Gleick's Chaos[6] tells the human story behind it.
